Bond or stock? Choosing the right risk measure for a convertible

Eva Liu, MS Mathematical Finance, Boston University

LinkedInevaliu3639@gmail.com Data as of 28 October 2026

Summary

A convertible bond is a corporate bond plus a call option on the issuer's stock. Depending on where the stock trades, it can behave like equity, like credit, or like both. I priced five US convertibles with a credit-adjusted binomial model, cleaned three years of FINRA TRACE trades, and backtested seven VaR methods to see which works when.

  • Fat tails sit in busted, bond-like convertibles (excess kurtosis 16.4, skew −0.89), not in equity-like ones (1.8). This reversed my starting hypothesis.
  • Sensitivity-based VaR misses issuer credit risk. For DexCom it gives 0.48% against 1.18% from the bond's own history, and an equity-only VaR was breached 39 times against 6 expected.
  • Treating a convertible as a straight bond (duration plus index spread) fails in every regime: 27–52% of days breach a 95% VaR.
  • In an equal-weight portfolio one bond is 20% of capital but 63% of risk. Under a 0.40–0.60 delta mandate, every optimizer lands near 14% volatility, so the delta target sets the risk.

The five bonds

The five bonds were chosen to cover every regime, each with at least a year of trade history and an issue size of $1.25 billion or more, so that TRACE prints are frequent. Terms come from the issuers' 8-K and 6-K filings. Premium is the bond price divided by parity, minus one, where parity is the value of the shares received on conversion. I call a day equity-like when the premium is below 15%, bond-like when it is above 80%, and balanced in between.

The same bond moves between regimes. ON Semi went from balanced to nearly equity-like in June 2026 and back again. Lumentum first traded at 107.6 in September 2025 and now has a delta of 0.98. The right risk tool therefore has to follow the regime rather than the name.

Data and cleaning

All inputs come from free public sources. Bond trades are from FINRA TRACE: 44,974 disseminated trades across 11 CUSIPs, because each bond began as a 144A note and later moved to a registered CUSIP, and I joined the two lines at the hand-off date. Terms are from SEC EDGAR, including later amendments and dividend adjustments to conversion rates. Equity prices are split-adjusted closes from Yahoo Finance; the Treasury curve, ICE BofA OAS indices and VIX are from FRED.

I removed 1,251 cancel and reversal reports together with the 1,248 original trades they matched, plus 457 weighted-average and portfolio trades. The daily price is the par-weighted average of trades of $100,000 or more. Days without trades are left empty.

Last-sale returns show negative first-order autocorrelation, down to −0.32, which is what bid-ask bounce looks like. Had I used them, Alibaba's volatility, and every VaR built on it, would have been about 80% too high. After cleaning, autocorrelation is close to zero.

Pricing model

Each bond is priced with the Tsiveriotis–Fernandes model. At every node of a binomial stock tree, value is split into an equity component, discounted at the risk-free rate because shares carry no issuer default risk, and a cash component, discounted at the risk-free rate plus the issuer's spread. The tree includes coupons, conversion, the soft call and Alibaba's 2029 put.

A single price cannot identify both volatility and credit spread. When the premium is above 50% the price mostly reflects credit, so I fix volatility at its 252-day realized level and solve for the spread. Otherwise I fix the spread at a reference ICE index and solve for implied volatility.

Three model problems found along the way

Unstable bump-and-reprice delta

The soft-call barrier falls between lattice nodes and moves when the stock is bumped, so finite-difference deltas jumped around: 0.68 at ±1% and 0.24 at ±2% for Super Micro. Reading delta and gamma from the tree's first nodes gave values stable to ±0.02 from 150 to 2,000 steps.

An over-aggressive soft call

The indenture requires the stock to be at 130% of the conversion price on 20 of 30 days, but the tree called on first touch, and ON Semi needed 130–190% implied volatility to fit. An effective 150% trigger cut calibration failures from 24 days to 1. Make-whole shares on redemption are not modelled; 13 days remain flagged.

New terms priced before they took effect

Super Micro amended its notes on 20 February 2025 after late SEC filings: coupon from 0% to 3.5%, conversion price from $134 to $83.44. The market priced the change from the 11 February announcement, which produced negative implied spreads until the model switched terms on that date.

Checks: with the stock near zero the model returns the straight-bond value (91.0600 against 91.0601 from discounted cash flows); deep in the money it returns close to parity; prices converge as the number of steps doubles.

Sensitivities

Effective duration and spread duration come from ±25 basis point shifts of the risk-free curve and of the issuer spread. Across 3,221 bond-days, no duration exceeds remaining life, every delta lies between 0 and 1, gamma is never negative, and delta falls as the premium rises for every bond.

Backtests by regime

Forecasts are one day ahead from rolling 250-day windows for a long position, and each day is assigned the regime it started in. Two methods deliberately see only half the instrument: equity only (delta times stock risk) and rate and credit (duration and index-spread moves).

I expected normal VaR to understate tail risk when a convertible trades like equity. The data show the opposite. Equity-like returns are volatile but close to normal, while bond-like returns have excess kurtosis of 16 and negative skew. Historical expected shortfall is 1.23 times the normal estimate in the bond-like regime, and 1.37 times for Super Micro.

Two patterns explain most of the misses. The first is wrong-way credit risk. On 20 March 2026 Super Micro's stock fell 40% and the busted bond lost 11.2%, while delta implied 7.0%, because the equity crash was itself a credit event. The second is asynchronous prices. DexCom reported after the close on 25 July 2024; institutional bond trades after 4 p.m. printed near 86, down from 97 earlier that day, while the stock's 41% fall appeared only in the next day's close.

Convexity: full revaluation versus a linear approximation

A long convertible has positive gamma. As the stock falls, delta falls with it and losses slow down, so a linear, delta-based VaR overstates the downside.

Portfolio construction

Portfolios are long-only and fully invested. I compared mean-variance optimization using historical mean returns with three risk-based methods that need no return forecast: minimum variance, minimum expected shortfall (solved as a Rockafellar–Uryasev linear program) and risk parity. Versions marked "-c" add limits a balanced convertible fund might use: 35% per issuer, portfolio delta between 0.40 and 0.60, and DTS of at most 300.

Mean-variance put 48% in Lumentum because it rose most in the sample. Across 300 bootstrap resamples of the 204 days its weights have standard deviations of 13 to 34 percentage points, and of resamples put more than 80% in a single bond. Unconstrained minimum variance does the opposite, putting 91% in DexCom with a delta of 0.18, which makes it a bond fund rather than a convertible fund.

Inside the delta limits, minimum variance, minimum expected shortfall and constrained risk parity all realize about 14% volatility, the same as equal weight. Raising average delta from 0.26 to 0.45 roughly doubles volatility. I would use constrained risk parity: it meets every limit, spreads risk most evenly, keeps turnover moderate and needs no return forecast.

Conclusion: which tool, when

At the portfolio level, set the equity exposure first and then allocate risk with a constrained risk-parity rule. Historical mean returns should not be used to size convertible positions.

Limitations and methods

  • Not modelled: contingent-conversion windows, make-whole shares on redemption, and issuer-specific spreads or CDS.
  • Rating buckets for unrated issuers are assumptions: Alibaba A; ON Semi and DexCom BBB; Lumentum BB; Super Micro B. ICE OAS history on FRED starts on 30 October 2023.
  • Lumentum has one year of history (120-day window, about 125 backtest days). The five-bond common sample is 204 days.
  • Bond prices are daily trade-weighted averages and stock prices are closes. Before 27 October 2023 (285 days for ON Semi and DexCom) only the end-of-day last sale is available.
  • Transaction costs are ignored; convertible bid-ask spreads can be 0.5 to 1 point.
